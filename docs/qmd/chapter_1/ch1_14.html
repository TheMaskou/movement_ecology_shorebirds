Movement & Time (Exploratory analysis)

Published

2026-09-30

In this section, we explore how bird detections are distributed over time.

A preliminary manual examination of the raw detection data indicated that most detections occurred during specific periods, particularly in the early morning and late evening.

We therefore aim to investigate how birds move within the estuary in relation to time of day; hourly instead of day/night, light conditions, and tidal cycle. Several environmental variables may be considered, including natural light levels during the day, represented by solar radiation, and at night, represented by moonlight. Tidal cycles obviously also influence bird movements, although it might drive shorebird movements after the circadian cycle.

An important limitation remains: most birds were detected during the day and around high tide. This pattern might partly reflect the placement of the Motus stations, which were located in areas where birds were known to occur. Consequently, it is difficult to determine whether the observed detection pattern reflects genuine site use or is partly a result of station placement.

Exploratory synthesis

CIRCADIAN OR TIDAL CYCLE?

The raw data table (one row = one detection; several consecutive detections = one run) is first processed using the “one-hour gap” rule, whereby any gap of less than one hour between two runs are systematically merged into a single and continuous run.

We refer to this merged run as a Passage. At this stage, all data is treated the same way, without distinguishing between Fly-Bys and Visits.

A Passage reflects how many times a bird has been detected by the station (count), versus how long it was continuously detected during each passage (duration). In other words How often versus How long each detection lasted.

Now, see below how the data behaves when we overlay circadian and tidal cycles. The detection suggests the birds are more active at dawn and dusk than during the day or the night (number of detections). In another hand, it seems birds settle somewhere during the day or night, while they just fly-by at dawn and dusk (duration of the detection).

However, it looks like most of the sites surveyed by our Motus array is used during the diurnal high tides, leaving nocturnal and low tide less documented or used.

Method review

Overall, the results obtained in the above are descriptive, yet highly validated by the Australasian Shorebirds Conference (2026) community, including academic researchers. Therefore, we account for some feedback and checks, see below.

Count vs Duration, no overlap

Unfold and check the method in details!

Following several feedback (Australasian Shorebirds Conference - 2026), we ensured the counts were not double-noted when the duration is over one hour. For example, if one visit starts at 04:00 am and last 2 hours 30 min, this visit should be counted only once at 04:00 am with a duration of 2,5 hours; and not as 3 visits with one of a duration of 1 hour at 04:00 am, another of 1 hour at 05:00 am and a last one of 0.5 hours (30 min) at 06:00 am.

To do so, we will manually work through one bird with a lot of detections, so a lot of visits, spanning for some several hours and others less than an hour.

This bird in our case is a Curlew sandpiper with the Band.ID = 4281407. Then, we run the exact same code we used throughout the analysis above.

First, we determine the duration of each runs, merging two runs when they are spaced less than one hour counting the number of visit and for each their duration.

Finally, we select one particular visit to see exactly what happens in our code, and what output it provides for review.

This visit has the id (visitID) = 51 and occurring at the station (recvDeployName) of Curlew point.

Auto-correlation, keep in mind

Unfold and see how count is correlated to duration!

As another feedback, a correlation is highly suspected between number of visits and the visit duration. For example, many visit must be shorts when few visit must be long.

In order to check this, we will plot below, collapsed into each hour bin (n = 24), the total number of visit (x axis) against the duration of these (y axis). The duration is once displayed as the mean and once as the median. Besides, a correlation test is applied to both the mean and the median scenarios.

We conclude there’s no evidence in this data-set that busier hours of the day (with a lot of visits) are associated with systematically shorter (or longer) visits.

Inferential analysis

Now we are convinced the circadian cycle plays a genuine role into the level of activity for shorebirds, and so their movement, we will model the shorebird activity (i.e., number of visit, the duration of the visits) to assess how dependent it is from (1) the level of natural light and (2) the tide height.

Model settings

Let’s start by settings few requirements for building several models.

Which explains shorebird visits better: natural light or tide? (1) number of visits per hour (table: grid, one row per bird x hour) (2) duration of each visit (table: vd, one row per visit) All species and receivers pooled.

Random effects: bird (Band.ID) and local day of visit (date).

We need to:

  • vd: have the duration of the visits for each visit and not rounded

  • grid: have for every bird, take every day it was detected at least once, and create all 24 hours of that day. Hours without a visit get n_visits = 0 (these zeros are essential: they tell the model when birds did NOT come).

  • have to keep only data with both light intensity/tide height predictors so all model run through same number of rows, a requirement to compare models (AIC/likelihood-ratio).

TZ <- "Australia/Sydney" # set for good

# Get 'vd'(as visit duration) but with one row per visit 
vd <- visit_duration %>%
  mutate(
    date  = factor(as.Date(visitStart, tz = TZ)),                       # to use as random effect
    dur_h = as.numeric(difftime(visitEnd, visitStart, units = "hours")),# exact duration in hours and not rounded
    dur_h = pmax(dur_h, 1 / 3600))                                      # at least 1 second (Gamma needs > 0)

# Get 'grid' (as number of visit) but with one row per bird*hour (for the NUMBER OF VISITS model)
to_utc <- function(x) as.POSIXct(format(x, tz = "UTC"), tz = "UTC")

bird_days <- distinct(vd, Band.ID, date)

make_hours <- function(d) { # get all 24 local hours of one day
  d <- as.Date(as.character(d))
  seq(as.POSIXct(paste(d,     "00:00"), tz = TZ),
      as.POSIXct(paste(d + 1, "00:00"), tz = TZ) - 3600, by = "hour")
  }

grid <- do.call(rbind, lapply(seq_len(nrow(bird_days)), function(i) # one row per bird x hour
  data.frame(Band.ID    = bird_days$Band.ID[i],
             date       = bird_days$date[i],
             hour_local = make_hours(bird_days$date[i]))))
grid$hour_utc <- to_utc(grid$hour_local)

# Number of visits STARTING in each bird-hour (0 if none)
vd$hour_utc <- as.POSIXct(trunc(to_utc(vd$visitStart), "hours"))
visits_per_hour <- count(vd, Band.ID, hour_utc, name = "n_visits")
grid <- left_join(grid, visits_per_hour, by = c("Band.ID", "hour_utc"))
grid$n_visits[is.na(grid$n_visits)] <- 0

# Light for each hour with one row per hour
light_start_utc <- as.POSIXct(light_h$hour_end_utc, format = "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M",
                              tz = "UTC") - 3600
grid$ambiant_lux_log <- light_h$ambiant_lux_log[match(as.numeric(grid$hour_utc),
                                                      as.numeric(light_start_utc))]

# Tide height at the middle of each hour 
tide_time_col <- "tideDateTimeAus"
tide_data <- tide_data[order(tide_data[[tide_time_col]]), ]
grid$tideHeight <- approx(x = as.numeric(tide_data[[tide_time_col]]), # linear interpolation of tide_data
                          y = tide_data$tideHeight,
                          xout = as.numeric(grid$hour_utc) + 1800,
                          ties = mean)$y

# Tide turning points
h    <- tide_data$tideHeight
prev <- c(NA, head(h, -1))                                   # height before
nxt  <- c(tail(h, -1), NA)                                   # height after
turn <- ifelse(h > prev & h >= nxt, "High",
        ifelse(h < prev & h <= nxt, "Low", NA))
tide_ev <- data.frame(t        = as.numeric(tide_data[[tide_time_col]]),
                      high_low = turn)[!is.na(turn), ]       # keep only high/low tides

# Diel category per  hour (row)
ev_hour <- as.numeric(trunc(as.POSIXct(tide_ev$t, origin = "1970-01-01", tz = "UTC"), "hours"))
tide_ev$day_night <- ifelse(light_h$is_day[match(ev_hour, as.numeric(light_start_utc))],
                            "Diurnal", "Nocturnal")
tide_ev$tideCategory <- paste(tide_ev$day_night, tide_ev$high_low, sep = "_")

# Nearest tide for each hour (grid) and each visit (vd)
nearest <- function(t) {
  i <- pmax(findInterval(t, tide_ev$t), 1)                   # tide just before
  j <- pmin(i + 1, nrow(tide_ev))                            # tide just after
  ifelse(abs(t - tide_ev$t[i]) <= abs(tide_ev$t[j] - t), i, j)
  }
lev_cat <- c("Diurnal_High", "Diurnal_Low", "Nocturnal_High", "Nocturnal_Low")

k <- nearest(as.numeric(grid$hour_utc) + 1800)               # grid: middle of each hour
grid$high_low     <- factor(tide_ev$high_low[k],     levels = c("High", "Low"))
grid$day_night    <- factor(tide_ev$day_night[k],    levels = c("Diurnal", "Nocturnal"))
grid$tideCategory <- factor(tide_ev$tideCategory[k], levels = lev_cat)

k <- nearest(as.numeric(vd$visitStart))                      # vd: start of each visit
vd$high_low     <- factor(tide_ev$high_low[k],     levels = c("High", "Low"))
vd$day_night    <- factor(tide_ev$day_night[k],    levels = c("Diurnal", "Nocturnal"))
vd$tideCategory <- factor(tide_ev$tideCategory[k], levels = lev_cat)

Model definitions

As suggested above, we want to test the activity of the birds (number of visit as in movements & duration of visits as in site use) and assess whether the tide or the light is first explaining it, including days and individuals as random effects.

We will build four different models and select the one depending its best likelihood ratio and AIC value: - Model null, as in maybe none of our predictors drive the activity of the birds - Model testing the light level only - Model testing the tide only - Model testing both light and tide (additional) - Model testing both light and tide (relation) (this one needs to be developped including two categorical variables for day/night and high/low)

We will repeat this twice, once for number of visits (movements) and once for duration of visit (site use).

OTHER TBC (AI HELP): This split works: 0.18 h (11 minutes) sits right in the dip between the two humps. The results now tell a clear and simple story.

  1. Is a visit a long stay (>11 min) or a short pass? Mostly light, and tide a little.

Per +1 SD of light (about 3.3 log units), the odds of a long stay double: ×1.97, z = 17.2. Per +1 SD of tide (about 0.30 m), the odds rise about 29%: ×1.29, z = 6.8. Both effects are significant, but light is about 2.7 times stronger on the standardised scale.

  1. Once a bird stays, how long does it stay? Only tide matters.

Light has no effect at all: ×1.00, p = 0.997. Each +1 SD of tide makes stays about 19% longer: ×1.19, p < 0.001.

Putting it together:

Light decides the type of visit. In the dark, birds make many short passes, which is also why the hourly visit count was higher at night. In daylight, visits are more often real stays. Tide decides how long those stays last. Higher tide means longer stays, which fits birds roosting on the site over high tide.

Residuals (the plot you sent, presumably d_len): acceptable. The QQ line is almost straight, and the quantile curves stay close to 0.25 / 0.5 / 0.75. The tests are flagged mainly because n = 2,008 makes DHARMa very sensitive. Some deviation is also expected because the model only sees durations above 11 minutes, so the lower tail is cut off. State that in your methods and it’s fine to report. Check that the d_long residual plot looks similar before using that model too.
